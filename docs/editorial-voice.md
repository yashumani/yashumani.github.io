# Editorial voice

This site should sound like one person explaining real work, not a brochure and not a generated report.

## Voice reference

The baseline voice is direct, curious, and practical. It is comfortable saying "I think," "I built this because," and "this part is not finished yet." It does not hide uncertainty behind formal language.

## Rules

1. Start with the real problem. Avoid a ceremonial opening.
2. Use concrete nouns and verbs. Prefer "the API checks the policy" to "policy enforcement is performed."
3. Keep technical terms when they carry meaning. Remove jargon that only makes a sentence sound important.
4. Use first person in essays and case studies when the judgment is mine.
5. Keep maturity boundaries visible. Say what works, what is proposed, and what still needs operations.
6. Vary sentence length. A short sentence is useful when it earns the emphasis.
7. Keep lists when they help the reader scan a system. Do not force every idea into three matching points.
8. Preserve facts, dates, metrics, links, and citations. Editing must not create new evidence.
9. Use simple punctuation. Prefer periods, commas, and colons to a run of em dashes.
10. End when the point is made. Do not restate the thesis in three different forms.

## Phrases to cut or rewrite

- "This paper proposes" or "This paper presents"
- "The more useful question"
- "It is important to note"
- "The goal is not X; it is Y"
- "not just X, but Y"
- "serves as," "stands as," or "showcases"
- generic transitions such as "Furthermore," "Moreover," and "Additionally"
- promotional claims that are not tied to evidence
- repeated groups of three written only for rhythm
- conclusions that summarize every section instead of landing one clear point

## Paper structure

A working paper should usually contain:

- a short personal or operational reason for writing it
- the claim in plain language
- the framework, examples, or evidence
- limits and unresolved questions
- references and a dated review note

The structure can change when the subject needs something else.

## Case-study structure

A case study should answer, in this order:

1. Why did I build it?
2. What works today?
3. How does the system work?
4. Which decisions mattered?
5. What remains unproven or externally dependent?
6. Where can the reader inspect the evidence?

## Source notes

This guide borrows useful editing checks from the public `blader/humanizer` Claude Code skill, especially its guidance on promotional language, vague attribution, repeated sentence shapes, em-dash overuse, rule-of-three overuse, and voice calibration. The final authority is the author's own writing sample and the facts in the source material.

"Ghost mode" is not treated as an official Anthropic writing skill. Online uses of that name range from informal shorthand for concise writing to commercial AI-detector products. This repository uses the legitimate editorial idea: remove filler, preserve meaning, and make ownership visible. It does not optimize copy to evade a detector.
