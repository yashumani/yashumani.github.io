Working paper / Business intelligence and finance

Metric Contracts for Decision-Grade Analytics

A practical semantic-governance framework for business intelligence and finance metrics across formula, grain, time, aggregation, ownership, quality, and lineage.

PublishedAugust 12, 2026Last reviewedAugust 12, 2026StatusWorking paper

Thesis. A metric becomes decision-grade only when its business meaning, technical behavior, ownership, quality requirements, and change history are governed together.

01Abstract

Business intelligence fails when a metric looks precise but carries ambiguous meaning. Two dashboards can calculate the same label at different grain, with different filters, time zones, target logic, currency treatment, and aggregation rules. The dispute is then framed as a data problem even though the missing layer is semantic governance.

This paper proposes a metric contract: a versioned, testable definition that connects a business question to formula, grain, time behavior, aggregation, ownership, lineage, quality requirements, and decision use.

02Why metric disputes happen

Most metric disagreements are not caused by arithmetic. They come from unstated assumptions:

  • Which event or accounting date determines the period?
  • Is the metric daily, transaction-level, customer-level, or monthly?
  • Are cancelled, reversed, internal, or incomplete records excluded?
  • Can the value be summed across time, entities, products, or currencies?
  • Is “target” budget, forecast, expected model output, prior year, or management commitment?
  • Does higher always mean better?

A semantic layer must answer these questions before visualization.

04Time semantics are part of the formula

Daily, week-to-date, month-to-date, quarter-to-date, year-to-date, trailing weeks, and rolling months are not display options applied after calculation. They are semantic transformations that can change the question.

A contract should define:

  • Fiscal and calendar boundaries.
  • Partial-period behavior and “as of” time.
  • Comparable prior periods, including leap years and 4-4-5 calendars where relevant.
  • Rolling-window length and whether the current incomplete period is included.
  • Restatement policy when late postings or corrections arrive.

For finance workflows, a 15-month trend, a trailing 13-week view, and current MTD variance should derive from the same governed metric but have separately declared period logic.

05Aggregation is not always addition

Metrics should be classified before rollup:

AdditiveCan be summed across all declared dimensions and time, such as many expense amounts.
Semi-additiveCan be summed across entities but not across time, such as period-end balances.
Non-additiveRequires recomputation, such as rates, percentages, medians, and distinct counts.
DerivedDepends on aligned component metrics, such as price-volume-mix or unit economics.

Ratios should preserve numerator and denominator. Currency measures should define translation timing. Snapshot measures should define which date survives aggregation.

06Actual, target, forecast, and expected are different objects

“Variance” has meaning only when both sides of the comparison are governed. Actual may mean posted, accrued, operational, or estimated. Target may mean budget, forecast, quota, prior year, statistical expectation, or threshold.

The contract should identify the comparison object, its version, approval state, grain compatibility, and refresh cadence. Without that, a variance chart can be numerically correct and decisionally wrong.

07Quality gates before insight

A metric should not automatically become analysis-ready because it exists. Quality checks should cover completeness, validity, conformity, consistency, uniqueness where relevant, integrity, privacy, period coverage, and mapping readiness.

Some dimensions cannot be proven from one file. Accuracy needs a trusted reference. Drift needs historical profiles. Timeliness needs expected arrival rules. Lineage needs source-system evidence. A good contract distinguishes measured checks from unresolved requirements.

08The operating model

Metric governance should fit the workflow rather than become a detached catalog project.

  1. Propose: analyst or owner submits the business question and draft contract.
  2. Reconcile: finance, operations, and data owners resolve formula, grain, and time behavior.
  3. Test: automated checks validate implementation against known examples.
  4. Publish: the approved version becomes available to dashboards and analysis services.
  5. Observe: usage, quality incidents, and definition disputes feed maintenance.
  6. Version: changes create explicit effective dates and compatibility notes.

09Application to investigation systems

A data-quality-aware anomaly workflow needs metric contracts for more than display labels. Polarity determines whether a positive residual is favorable. Grain determines whether additive attribution is valid. Hierarchy determines legal drill paths. The target contract determines whether Actual − Expected is meaningful.

The Drill-down Anomaly Lab currently makes several boundaries explicit: median fallback is exploratory rather than predictive, ratio attribution requires numerator/denominator awareness, and accuracy or drift needs external evidence. Those limitations are not weaknesses to hide; they are contract requirements for the next production layer.

  "polarity": "lower_is_better",
  "time_basis": "fiscal_calendar",
  "aggregation": "additive_after_currency_translation",
  "quality_gate": ["complete_period", "mapped_entity", "valid_currency"]

The exact schema can vary. The important point is that semantic decisions become machine-readable enough to drive calculation, labeling, tests, drill paths, and explanations consistently.

11Conclusion

A dashboard is not the semantic layer. A calculation is not a contract. Decision-grade analytics requires a maintained agreement between business meaning and technical implementation.

Metric contracts make definitions visible, time behavior explicit, aggregation safe, quality enforceable, and changes traceable. They are the missing interface between finance ownership and analytical automation.
